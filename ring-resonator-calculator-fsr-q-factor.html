Formula

L = 2πR    FSR = λ² / (n_g L)    a = exp(−αL/2), r = √(1 − κ²)
all-pass: FWHM = (1 − ra) λ² / (π n_g L √(ra)),   T_min = (a − r)² / (1 − ra)²
add-drop: replace ra by r²a; drop at resonance = (1 − r²)² a / (1 − r²a)²
Q_loaded = λ / FWHM    Q_int = 2π n_g / (λ α)    finesse = FSR / FWHM

Worked example

A 10 µm radius silicon ring (n_g = 4.2) with 2 dB/cm loss and κ² = 2 % at 1550 nm: L = 62.83 µm, FSR = 1.55² / (4.2 × 62.83) µm = 9.10 nm, the all-pass linewidth is 33.5 pm, so the loaded Q is 4.6 × 10⁴ while the intrinsic Q is 3.7 × 10⁵. The ring is strongly over-coupled (r = 0.990 < a = 0.9986), so the dip is only 2.5 dB deep.

How it works

Microrings are the workhorse of silicon and silicon-nitride photonics: filters, modulators, sensors and frequency combs. The free spectral range is set by the circumference and group index; the linewidth is set by how much light leaks out per round trip, both into the bus waveguide (coupling) and into loss.

The deepest through-port dip happens at critical coupling (r = a), where coupling equals loss. Over-coupled rings (more coupling than loss) have broad, shallow dips; under-coupled rings have narrow, shallow dips. Use the group index, not the effective index, for FSR and linewidth.

FAQ

Which index should I use?

The group index n_g (from a mode solver or a measured FSR). Silicon strip waveguides at 1550 nm are around 4.2; silicon nitride is around 2.

How do I get the intrinsic Q from a measurement?

Fit the loaded Q and extinction, decide whether you are under- or over-coupled, then solve for a. This tool goes the other way, from design values.

Why is my measured FSR different?

Dispersion of n_g and the exact path length (including straight coupler sections in racetracks) change it.
