Worked example

With a 532 nm laser (ν̃₀ = 18,796.99 cm⁻¹) the 520.7 cm⁻¹ silicon phonon appears on the Stokes side at 10⁷ / 18,276.29 = 547.157 nm (547.005 nm in air) and on the anti-Stokes side at 517.660 nm. The shift corresponds to 15.610 THz and 64.559 meV. At 295 K the anti-Stokes line is (19,317.69 / 18,276.29)⁴ × exp(−1.43878 × 520.7 / 295) = 1.2482 × 0.07890 = 0.0985 times as strong as the Stokes line for a power detector, or 0.0932 if you count photons. Going the other way, a measured ratio of 0.100 means 296.8 K (power) or 303.5 K (photon counting), which shows how much the choice of exponent matters. Diamond's 1332.5 cm⁻¹ line excited at 532 nm sits at 572.590 nm (Stokes) and 496.784 nm (anti-Stokes). With a 785 nm laser a 1000 cm⁻¹ band lands at 851.872 nm, where the dispersion is 13.78 cm⁻¹ per nm; a spectrometer with 0.1 nm per pixel and 1024 pixels centred there covers 249 to 1666 cm⁻¹. In inverse mode, 550.00 nm measured with a 532 nm laser is a Stokes shift of 615.17 cm⁻¹, and 517.66 nm is an anti-Stokes shift of 520.71 cm⁻¹.

How it works

In Raman scattering a photon from the laser gives up (Stokes) or picks up (anti-Stokes) one quantum of a vibration of the sample, so the scattered light is shifted in energy by exactly the vibrational energy. Spectra are therefore plotted against the Raman shift in wavenumbers (cm⁻¹), which is the same for a given band whatever the laser. A detector, edge filter or spectrometer grating, however, works in wavelength, and because the shift is a difference of reciprocal wavelengths you cannot simply subtract nanometres: the same 1000 cm⁻¹ band is 29.9 nm from a 532 nm laser but 66.9 nm from a 785 nm laser. This calculator converts either way, gives both the Stokes and anti-Stokes lines and expresses the shift as a frequency and an energy.

Spectrometers and laser data sheets often quote air wavelengths (a HeNe is 632.816 nm in air, 632.991 nm in vacuum). The difference of about 0.03 % (0.17 nm at 633 nm) is several cm⁻¹ in absolute wavenumber, although it largely cancels in the shift when both wavelengths are given in the same medium. Choose whether your inputs are in air or vacuum; the tool converts internally with the IAU standard-air formula and reports both.

The ratio of anti-Stokes to Stokes intensity follows the Boltzmann population of the excited vibrational level, which makes it a contact-free thermometer: the tool predicts the ratio at a temperature and inverts a measured ratio to a temperature. The frequency factor is the fourth power of the scattered frequency for a detector that measures power, but the third power for a photon-counting CCD or PMT, which shifts the answer by several kelvin. Limits: the ratio assumes the same detection efficiency on both sides (correct your measured ratio for the instrument response and filter transmission first), no resonance enhancement, and no absorption or self-heating differences between the two lines. Band positions themselves move with temperature, stress and doping (silicon by about −0.02 cm⁻¹ per °C), so the presets are room-temperature reference values. The dispersion rows convert a spectrometer's nm per pixel at the Stokes line into cm⁻¹ per pixel and the Raman range a detector of that width covers; use the diffraction grating calculator for the grating geometry itself.

FAQ

How do I convert a Raman shift in cm⁻¹ to wavelength in nm?

λ_Stokes = 10⁷ / (10⁷/λ₀ − Δν̃) with λ in nm. For a 785 nm laser and a 1000 cm⁻¹ band: 10⁷/785 = 12,738.85 cm⁻¹, minus 1000 gives 11,738.85 cm⁻¹, so λ = 851.87 nm. The anti-Stokes line is at 10⁷ / (12,738.85 + 1000) = 727.86 nm.

How do I calculate the Raman shift from the measured wavelength?

Δν̃ = 10⁷ (1/λ₀ − 1/λ_s) in cm⁻¹ with both wavelengths in nm. For 532 nm excitation and a peak at 550 nm: 18,796.99 − 18,181.82 = 615.17 cm⁻¹. A negative result means an anti-Stokes line.

Why is the anti-Stokes line weaker than the Stokes line?

Anti-Stokes scattering needs a molecule or crystal already in an excited vibrational state, and the Boltzmann factor exp(−hcΔν̃/kT) makes such states rare at room temperature: for the 520.7 cm⁻¹ silicon line at 295 K the anti-Stokes line is only about 10 % of the Stokes line, for diamond at 1332.5 cm⁻¹ about 0.3 %.

How do I measure temperature from the anti-Stokes/Stokes ratio?

T = (hc/k) Δν̃ / ln[((ν̃₀ + Δν̃)/(ν̃₀ − Δν̃))^p / R], where hc/k = 1.43878 cm·K, R is the measured ratio after correcting for the instrument response, and p is 4 for power detection or 3 for a photon-counting CCD. With 532 nm excitation and the silicon line, R = 0.10 means about 297 K (p = 4) or 303 K (p = 3).

What is the Raman shift of silicon?

The first-order optical phonon of crystalline silicon is at 520.7 cm⁻¹ at room temperature (often quoted as 520.7 ± 0.5 cm⁻¹ for wafer calibration). It drops by about 0.02 cm⁻¹ per °C, and stress and heavy doping also move it, so use low laser power when calibrating.

Should I use air or vacuum wavelengths?

Raman shifts are defined from vacuum wavenumbers, but many spectrometers are calibrated in air. If both wavelengths are in the same medium the shift changes by well under 1 cm⁻¹ in the visible; mixing an air laser wavelength with a vacuum scattered wavelength gives errors of several cm⁻¹. Set the input medium above to match your data.

How many cm⁻¹ per pixel does my spectrometer have?

Multiply the dispersion in nm per pixel by 10⁷/λ_s². At 851.9 nm (a 1000 cm⁻¹ shift from 785 nm) 0.1 nm per pixel is 1.38 cm⁻¹ per pixel; at 547 nm (532 nm excitation) 0.05 nm per pixel is 1.67 cm⁻¹ per pixel.
