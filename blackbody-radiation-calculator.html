Worked example

The Sun's photosphere (effective temperature 5772 K) peaks at 2897.772 / 5772 = 0.5020 µm, i.e. 502.0 nm in the green, and emits σT⁴ = 62.94 MW/m² (6294 W/cm²). 46.50 % of that power, but only 28.75 % of the photons, falls in the visible band 380–780 nm. A 2700 K incandescent filament peaks at 1.073 µm in the infrared and puts only about 8.4 % of a blackbody's output into 380–780 nm, which is why such lamps are inefficient. A 300 K room-temperature object peaks at 9.659 µm and emits 459.3 W/m²; 37.57 % of it (172.6 W/m²) lies in the 8–14 µm thermal-camera window, against 1.28 % (5.86 W/m²) in the 3–5 µm mid-wave window. At 10 µm its spectral radiance is 9.924 W·m⁻²·sr⁻¹·µm⁻¹. Human skin at 37 °C with ε ≈ 0.98 radiates 514.2 W/m², about 925 W from 1.8 m² of skin before subtracting what it absorbs back from the room.

How it works

Every object above absolute zero emits thermal radiation. An ideal blackbody, one that absorbs everything that falls on it, emits a spectrum that depends only on its temperature and is given by Planck's law. The spectrum has a single peak that moves to shorter wavelengths as the temperature rises (Wien's displacement law, λmaxT = 2897.8 µm·K), and the area under it grows as T⁴ (the Stefan-Boltzmann law). This is why a hot stove glows dull red, a filament glows yellow-white and the Sun looks white, while people and rooms at about 300 K emit almost entirely in the mid-infrared near 10 µm.

The calculator evaluates Planck's law with the exact SI 2019 values of h, c and k, so σ, the Wien constant and the photon constant are computed rather than typed in. The in-band fraction, the part of the total power between two wavelengths, is evaluated with the standard exponential series of the Planck integral (and a Bernoulli-number series at long wavelengths), which agrees with direct numerical integration to at least 14 significant digits. The photon fraction is computed separately because photon detectors such as photodiodes, CCDs and HgCdTe or InSb arrays count photons, not watts, and the two fractions differ a lot. Note that the wavelength peak (b/T) and the frequency peak (2.821 kT/h) are not at the same place: for the Sun the frequency peak is at 339.3 THz, which corresponds to 883 nm.

Limits: the emissivity is treated as constant with wavelength (a grey body). Real materials such as polished metals, tungsten, glass and many paints have emissivity that varies strongly with wavelength, so their spectra are only approximately grey. The exitance is what the surface emits into a hemisphere (Lambertian), not the net heat transfer: for that you subtract the radiation absorbed from the surroundings, ε σ (T⁴ − Tsurr⁴) for a small grey body in a large enclosure. Radiance is per steradian and assumes a Lambertian emitter.

FAQ

What is Wien's displacement law?

The wavelength of maximum spectral radiance is λ_max = b / T with b = 2897.772 µm·K. At 300 K that is 9.66 µm, at 1000 K 2.90 µm and at 5772 K (the Sun) 502 nm.

What is the Stefan-Boltzmann law?

The total power radiated per unit area by a blackbody is M = σT⁴, with σ = 5.670374 × 10⁻⁸ W·m⁻²·K⁻⁴. A grey body with emissivity ε radiates εσT⁴. At 300 K a blackbody radiates 459 W/m².

How much of a blackbody's power is in the 8–14 µm band?

At 300 K about 37.6 %. Enter the band edges to get the fraction for any temperature; it is the standard way to estimate the signal reaching a long-wave infrared (thermal) camera.

Why does the peak in frequency differ from the peak in wavelength?

Because the spectrum per unit wavelength and per unit frequency are different densities (dν = c dλ / λ²). The frequency peak is at ν = 2.821 kT/h, which for the Sun is 339 THz (883 nm), not the 502 nm wavelength peak. Neither is more correct; quote the one that matches how your spectrum is plotted.

How many photons does a blackbody emit?

The photon exitance is 1.5205 × 10¹⁵ T³ photons per second per m² for ε = 1, so about 4.1 × 10²² photons·s⁻¹·m⁻² at 300 K. The calculator also gives the in-band photon flux, which is what a photon-counting detector sees.

Does this work for the Sun or stars?

Yes, as a first approximation. A star's spectrum is roughly a blackbody at its effective temperature (5772 K for the Sun), but absorption lines and limb darkening make the real spectrum deviate, especially in the ultraviolet.
