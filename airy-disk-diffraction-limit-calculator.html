Worked example

Green light at 550 nm through a 25 mm aperture with a 250 mm focal length (f/10, NA 0.05): the first dark ring of the Airy pattern is at r = 1.21967 × 0.55 µm × 10 = 6.71 µm, so the Airy disk is 13.42 µm across, the central peak is 5.66 µm FWHM and the second dark ring is at 12.28 µm. The Abbe limit is 0.55 / 0.1 = 5.50 µm. The same 25 mm aperture resolves 1.21967 × 550 nm / 25 mm = 26.8 µrad = 5.53 arcsec on the sky. The incoherent MTF cuts off at 1 / (0.55 µm × 10) = 182 lp/mm, so a sensor needs pixels of 2.75 µm or less to sample it at Nyquist; 2.4 µm pixels put 5.6 pixels across the Airy disk. A 1.4 NA oil-immersion objective at 500 nm gives r = 0.60983 × 0.5 / 1.4 = 0.218 µm.

How it works

Even a perfect lens cannot focus light from a point to a point. A uniformly illuminated circular aperture produces the Airy pattern: a bright central disk holding 83.8 % of the energy, surrounded by fainter rings. The radius of the first dark ring, 1.22 λ N, sets the scale. By the Rayleigh criterion, two equally bright incoherent points are just resolved when the peak of one falls on the first dark ring of the other, so the Airy radius is also the resolution limit. The same physics gives a telescope its angular resolution of 1.22 λ / D and limits a microscope objective to about 0.61 λ / NA.

The f-number N = f / D and the numerical aperture are two descriptions of the same cone of light; in the paraxial limit NA = 1 / (2N). Enter the focal length and aperture diameter for camera lenses and telescopes, or the NA for microscope objectives, where it already includes the immersion index. The Abbe limit λ / (2NA) is the period of the finest grating that can be imaged and is slightly smaller than the Rayleigh distance. For digital imaging, the incoherent modulation transfer function falls to zero at 1 / (λN) line pairs per mm, so pixels of λN / 2 or smaller sample everything the lens can deliver; larger pixels mean the sensor, not diffraction, limits sharpness.

Limits: the results assume an aberration-free optic with a uniformly illuminated, unobstructed circular pupil, incoherent light and focus in air (or NA entered including the medium). Real lenses are only diffraction-limited when their aberrations are small (Strehl ratio above about 0.8). A central obstruction, as in most reflecting telescopes, narrows the core and moves energy into the rings. A Gaussian laser beam does not form an Airy pattern; use the focused spot size calculator for it. The paraxial NA = D / (2f) is accurate to about 1 % up to NA 0.15.

FAQ

What is the Airy disk formula?

The radius of the Airy disk to its first dark ring is r = 1.22 λ N, where N = f / D is the f-number, or equivalently r = 0.61 λ / NA. Its diameter is 2.44 λ N; at 550 nm that is about 1.34 µm per unit f-number, so f/8 gives a 10.7 µm Airy disk.

What is the difference between the Rayleigh and Abbe limits?

Rayleigh, 0.61 λ / NA, is the separation at which two incoherent point images are just resolved (peak on the first dark ring). Abbe, λ / (2NA) = 0.5 λ / NA, is the finest periodic structure an objective can transmit. They differ by a factor of 1.22.

How do I calculate the angular resolution of a telescope?

θ = 1.22 λ / D in radians. Multiply by 206 265 for arcseconds: a 100 mm telescope at 550 nm resolves about 1.38 arcsec, close to the empirical Dawes limit of 116 / D[mm] = 1.16 arcsec.

At what f-number does my camera become diffraction-limited?

When the Airy disk grows to about two to three pixels across, diffraction starts to soften fine detail. With 4 µm pixels at 550 nm the Airy diameter 1.34 µm × N reaches 8–12 µm at about f/6 to f/9.

Why is the Airy disk different from a laser spot size?

The Airy pattern comes from a flat-top (uniformly lit) aperture. A Gaussian laser beam focuses to a Gaussian spot with no rings, quoted at the 1/e² diameter 4λf / (πD); the two formulas describe different input beams.
