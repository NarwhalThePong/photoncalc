Worked example

A zero-order quartz quarter-wave plate for the sodium D line (λ = 589.3 nm, Δn = 1.5534 − 1.5443 = 0.0091) is only 0.25 × 589.3 / 0.0091 = 16.19 µm thick. With its fast axis at 45° to linearly polarized input it gives circular light (|S₃| = 1, axis ratio 1), and any analyzer passes 50 % of the power. Used at 633 nm instead it gives 0.2327 waves (83.8°), still close to a quarter wave. A third-order plate (m = 3) is 210.46 µm thick and 3.25 waves at 589.3 nm, but at 633 nm it gives 3.0256 waves, an effective 0.026 wave, so the light comes out almost linear. That is why the wavelength shift that moves the retardance by λ/100 is 23.6 nm for the zero-order plate and only 1.81 nm for the multi-order one. A zero-order half-wave plate (32.38 µm) with its axis at 22.5° rotates the polarization by 45°, so an analyzer at 45° transmits 100 %. With no retardance (a full-wave plate) an analyzer at 60° passes cos²60° = 25 %, which is Malus's law.

How it works

A wave plate (retarder) is a slab of birefringent crystal cut so that light polarized along its fast axis travels faster than light polarized along its slow axis. After a thickness t the two components are out of step by the retardance Γ = 2π Δn t / λ. A quarter-wave plate (Γ = 90°) turns linear polarization at 45° to its axes into circular polarization and back; a half-wave plate (Γ = 180°) rotates linear polarization by twice the angle between the input and the fast axis, which makes it the standard tool for rotating a laser's polarization or, with a polarizer, for a variable attenuator.

Only the retardance modulo one wave matters for the polarization, so a plate of 3.25 waves acts like a quarter-wave plate at its design wavelength. Such multi-order plates are thicker and cheaper but much more sensitive to wavelength, temperature and angle, because the whole retardance scales with 1/λ. Zero-order plates are either a single thin plate (tens of µm of quartz) or two thick plates with crossed axes whose difference is the target thickness, which this tool treats the same way: enter the net thickness. The calculator gives the thickness for a target, or the retardance of a plate you have, at the design and at a second wavelength, and then the polarization state that comes out when linearly polarized light enters with the fast axis at θ. The output is described by the normalized Stokes parameters, the ellipticity and the orientation of the polarization ellipse, and the fraction that passes an ideal analyzer at angle φ (Malus's law is the special case with no plate).

Limits: the birefringence is taken as constant, so the second-wavelength result ignores the dispersion of Δn (a few percent across the visible for quartz). Normal incidence, an ideal lossless retarder and an ideal analyzer are assumed; reflection losses and the extinction ratio of real polarizers are not included. The handedness of circular light depends on the sign of Δn and on the convention used, so only |S₃| is reported.

FAQ

How thick is a quarter-wave plate?

t = (m + 1/4) λ / Δn. For crystal quartz (Δn ≈ 0.0091) at 589 nm a zero-order plate is about 16.2 µm thick; for calcite (Δn ≈ 0.172) only 0.86 µm, which is why calcite is rarely used for wave plates.

What is the difference between zero-order and multi-order wave plates?

A zero-order plate has a total retardance of exactly the target (for example 0.25 waves); a multi-order plate has whole extra waves (for example 3.25). Both act the same at the design wavelength, but the multi-order plate drifts much faster with wavelength and temperature.

What is Malus's law?

The intensity passed by an ideal polarizer is I = I₀ cos²φ, where φ is the angle between the incoming linear polarization and the polarizer axis. At 60° that is 25 %, at 90° zero.

How does a half-wave plate rotate polarization?

If the input polarization makes an angle θ with the fast axis, the output is linear at 2θ from the input. A half-wave plate at 22.5° rotates the polarization by 45°, at 45° by 90°.

How do I make circularly polarized light?

Send linearly polarized light through a quarter-wave plate with its fast axis at 45° to the polarization. At other angles the output is elliptical; this calculator gives its ellipticity and axis ratio.

Why does a quarter-wave plate only work at one wavelength?

The retardance Δn t / λ changes as 1/λ (and Δn itself is dispersive). A zero-order quartz plate stays within λ/100 of a quarter wave over about ±24 nm near 589 nm, a third-order plate over only about ±1.8 nm.
