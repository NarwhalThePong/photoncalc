Formula

z_R = π w₀² / (M² λ)    depth of focus = 2 z_R    NA ≈ D / (2f)

Worked example

A 2 mm, M² = 1.1 beam at 1064 nm expanded 5× to 10 mm and focused by a 100 mm lens: 2w₀ = 4 × 1.1 × 1.064 µm × 100 mm / (π × 10 mm) = 14.9 µm, with a Rayleigh range of 149 µm (depth of focus 298 µm) at NA 0.05.

How it works

A lens focuses a collimated Gaussian beam to a waist whose size scales with the focal length and inversely with the beam diameter at the lens. That is why beam expanders are used before focusing optics: expanding the beam 5× shrinks the focused spot 5×, at the cost of a 25× shorter depth of focus.

The formula assumes the input waist sits near the lens and the lens is well corrected and paraxial. Above about NA 0.25, spherical aberration and non-paraxial effects make the real spot larger, and a beam clipped by the aperture (lens diameter under about 1.5× the 1/e² diameter) also spreads it.

FAQ

Why does a bigger beam give a smaller spot?

Diffraction: the far-field angle of a wave is λ/(π w), so a wider beam converges at a steeper angle and makes a tighter waist.

How does M² change the spot?

The spot diameter scales linearly with M², so an M² = 1.3 laser focuses 30 % larger than an ideal TEM₀₀ beam.

Is this the same as the Airy disk?

No. The Airy disk is for a uniformly lit (flat-top) aperture; a Gaussian beam focuses to a Gaussian waist, usually quoted at the 1/e² diameter.
