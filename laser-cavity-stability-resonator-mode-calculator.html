Formula

g₁ = 1 − L/R₁,   g₂ = 1 − L/R₂    (flat mirror: g = 1)    stable if 0 < g₁g₂ < 1
w₁² = (Lλ/πn) √[g₂ / (g₁(1 − g₁g₂))],   w₂² = (Lλ/πn) √[g₁ / (g₂(1 − g₁g₂))]
w₀² = (Lλ/πn) √[g₁g₂(1 − g₁g₂)] / |g₁ + g₂ − 2g₁g₂|,   waist at d₁ = L g₂(1 − g₁) / (g₁ + g₂ − 2g₁g₂) from mirror 1
FSR = c / (2nL),   ΔνT = FSR · arccos(±√(g₁g₂)) / π   (+ if g₁, g₂ > 0, − if < 0)
F = π (R₁R₂)1/4 / (1 − √(R₁R₂)),   δν = FSR / F,   τp = 1 / (2π δν),   Q = ν / δν

Worked example

A plano-concave Nd:YAG-style resonator, 250 mm long with a flat mirror and a 500 mm concave mirror at 1064 nm, has g₁ = 1, g₂ = 0.5 and g₁g₂ = 0.5, so it is stable. The TEM₀₀ waist sits on the flat mirror with w₀ = 291.0 µm, the spot on the curved mirror is √2 larger at 411.5 µm, the Rayleigh range is √(L(R − L)) = 250 mm and the far-field divergence is 1.164 mrad. The free spectral range is c/(2L) = 599.6 MHz, and because arccos(√0.5) = 45° the transverse modes sit exactly a quarter of an FSR apart (149.9 MHz). With a 99.9 % high reflector and a 95 % output coupler the finesse is 120.1, the cold-cavity linewidth 4.99 MHz, the photon lifetime 31.9 ns and Q = 5.65 × 10⁷. A symmetric cavity with two 1500 mm mirrors 1000 mm apart (g = 1/3) has a 489.4 µm waist in the middle and 599.4 µm spots on the mirrors. Two 400 mm mirrors 1000 mm apart give g₁g₂ = 2.25: unstable, no Gaussian mode.

How it works

A laser resonator only traps a beam if the mirrors keep refocusing it. For two spherical mirrors a distance L apart, everything about the geometry is captured by the g-parameters g = 1 − L/R, and the cavity is stable when the product g₁g₂ lies between 0 and 1. Inside that band, a unique Gaussian beam fits between the mirrors: its wavefront curvature matches each mirror's radius, which fixes the waist size, where the waist sits, and the spot size on each mirror. Outside it, rays walk off the mirrors after a few round trips (an unstable resonator, used deliberately in some high-gain lasers but with no Gaussian mode).

The calculator gives the TEM₀₀ mode (the 1/e² intensity radius, as used in the Gaussian beam calculator), the free spectral range between longitudinal modes, and the transverse mode spacing, which follows from the round-trip Gouy phase. The transverse spacing tells you whether higher-order modes fall on top of longitudinal ones: in a confocal cavity (R = L) they land at exactly half an FSR, which is why confocal scanning Fabry-Pérot interferometers are insensitive to mode matching. Near the stability edges (plane-parallel, hemispherical, concentric) the mode becomes very large or very small and the cavity becomes sensitive to misalignment; the calculator flags these marginal cases. Optional mirror reflectivities give the passive (cold-cavity) finesse, linewidth, photon lifetime and Q; for a fuller treatment of a Fabry-Pérot, see the Fabry-Pérot calculator.

Sign convention: a mirror that is concave toward the inside of the cavity has a positive radius, a convex one negative, and a flat mirror is entered as 0 (infinite radius). The intracavity index n assumes the cavity is uniformly filled (the wavelength in the medium is λ/n); for a short crystal inside an air-spaced cavity this is only an approximation, and thermal lensing in the gain medium is not included. Mirror reflectivities count only the mirror losses, not scattering or absorption inside the cavity, and the finesse uses the standard π√r/(1 − r) form, which is within 0.003 % of the exact Airy-function width for reflectivities above 95 %. With a convex mirror the waist can fall outside the cavity (a negative distance, or one larger than L, is a virtual waist behind a mirror).

FAQ

What is the stability condition for a laser cavity?

0 ≤ g₁g₂ ≤ 1 with g = 1 − L/R for each mirror. For example a 250 mm cavity with a flat mirror and a 500 mm mirror has g₁g₂ = 1 × 0.5 = 0.5 and is stable; two 400 mm mirrors 1 m apart give 2.25 and are unstable. The edges 0 and 1 are only marginally stable.

Where is the beam waist in a laser cavity?

At d₁ = L g₂(1 − g₁) / (g₁ + g₂ − 2g₁g₂) from mirror 1. In a symmetric cavity it is in the middle; in a plano-concave cavity it is on the flat mirror.

How big is the TEM00 mode in a confocal cavity?

For the symmetric confocal cavity (R = L) the waist is w₀ = √(Lλ/2π) and the spot on each mirror is √2 w₀. At 1064 nm and L = 500 mm that is 291 µm at the waist and 412 µm on the mirrors, the smallest mirror spots of any symmetric cavity of that length.

What is the transverse mode spacing of a laser cavity?

Δν_T = (c/2nL) · arccos(±√(g₁g₂)) / π. It is a quarter of the FSR for g₁g₂ = 0.5 and half the FSR for a confocal cavity, where the even and odd transverse modes fall on the longitudinal-mode comb and at its midpoints.

How do I enter a flat or convex mirror?

Enter 0 for a flat mirror (infinite radius, g = 1). A mirror that curves away from the cavity (convex) has a negative radius, which makes g larger than 1.

How do finesse and linewidth relate to the mirror reflectivities?

For mirror losses only, F = π(R₁R₂)^¼ / (1 − √(R₁R₂)) and the linewidth is FSR/F. With 99.9 % and 95 % mirrors F ≈ 120; the photon lifetime is 1/(2π × linewidth).
