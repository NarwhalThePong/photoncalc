Worked example

Standard SMF-28 at 1550 nm has D ≈ 17 ps/(nm·km), so β2 = −17 ps/(nm·km) × (1550 nm)² / (2πc) ≈ −21.7 ps²/km. Over 50 km the link accumulates 850 ps/nm, so a source 0.1 nm wide spreads by about 85 ps. A 10 ps transform-limited Gaussian pulse has T0 ≈ 6.0 ps and LD ≈ 1.66 km, so after 50 km it is about 30 times wider, roughly 301 ps FWHM.

How it works

Chromatic dispersion means different wavelengths travel at different group velocities, so a pulse that contains a range of wavelengths spreads out in time. Fiber datasheets quote it as the dispersion parameter D in ps/(nm·km): the delay difference, per nanometre of spectral width, per kilometre of fiber. Nonlinear-optics and ultrafast work instead uses the group-velocity dispersion β2 = d²β/dω² in ps²/km. The two are related by β2 = −Dλ²/(2πc), so positive D (the anomalous regime of standard fiber above about 1310 nm) means negative β2.

The calculator gives two broadening estimates. The source-width spread |D|·L·Δλ applies when the spectrum is much wider than the pulse's own transform limit, for example a directly modulated or multimode laser. The Gaussian-pulse result applies to a transform-limited, unchirped Gaussian pulse whose bandwidth comes from the pulse itself; it uses the dispersion length LD, the distance over which the pulse widens by √2.

Both use only second-order dispersion. Near the zero-dispersion wavelength (about 1310 nm in SMF-28), third-order dispersion β3 dominates and these numbers underestimate the broadening. Chirped pulses, fiber nonlinearity (self-phase modulation, solitons) and polarization-mode dispersion are not included.

FAQ

How do I convert D in ps/(nm·km) to β₂ in ps²/km?

Use β₂ = −Dλ²/(2πc). At 1550 nm, β₂ ≈ −1.275 × D, so 17 ps/(nm·km) is about −21.7 ps²/km.

What is the dispersion of SMF-28 at 1550 nm?

About 17–18 ps/(nm·km), with the zero-dispersion wavelength near 1310 nm. Corning specifies ≤ 18 ps/(nm·km) at 1550 nm for SMF-28 Ultra.

What is the dispersion length?

L_D = T₀²/|β₂|, with T₀ the 1/e half-width of the intensity (T_FWHM ≈ 1.665 T₀). After one dispersion length an unchirped Gaussian pulse is √2 times wider. Shorter pulses have much shorter dispersion lengths, since L_D scales with the square of the pulse width.

Does a negative D make pulses shorter?

Not for an unchirped pulse: its width grows the same way with either sign of β₂. The sign matters for chirped pulses, which is why dispersion-compensating fiber with negative D can undo the broadening from standard fiber.
