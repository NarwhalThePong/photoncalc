Worked example

A waveguide with 2 dB/cm propagation loss has α = 0.230259 × 2 = 0.4605 cm⁻¹. Over 5 mm the loss is 1 dB, an absorbance (OD) of 0.1, so 10−0.1 = 79.43 % of the light gets through: 7.943 mW of 10 mW. The 1/e depth is 21.7 mm and half the power is gone after 15.05 mm. In a 1 cm cuvette, NADH at 340 nm (ε = 6220 L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹) at 50 µmol/L gives A = 6220 × 5×10⁻⁵ × 1 = 0.311 and T = 48.87 %. Crystalline silicon at 632.8 nm has κ ≈ 0.019, so α = 4π × 0.019 / 632.8 nm = 3773 cm⁻¹: red light penetrates 2.65 µm and a 5 µm layer transmits 15.2 % (ignoring surface reflection). An OD 3 neutral-density filter transmits 0.1 %, which is 30 dB, or α = 34.5 cm⁻¹ if it is 2 mm thick.

How it works

Light travelling through an absorbing medium loses the same fraction of its power in every equal step, so the power falls exponentially: I = I₀ e−αL. This is the Beer-Lambert (or Bouguer-Lambert-Beer) law. Different fields quote the same attenuation in different units, which is where most mistakes come from. Physicists and semiconductor people use the Napierian absorption coefficient α in cm⁻¹; chemists and filter makers use the decadic absorbance or optical density A = log₁₀(I₀/I), which for a solution equals ε c L; photonics and fiber engineers quote loss in dB/cm, dB/m or dB/km, which is simply 10 A per unit length. One dB/cm is 0.2303 cm⁻¹, and α in cm⁻¹ is 4.343 dB/cm. The imaginary part κ of the complex refractive index n + iκ is tied to α by α = 4πκ/λ₀, which lets you turn tabulated optical constants into a penetration depth.

The calculator converts whatever you know into all of these, then applies the path length you enter. The penetration depth 1/α is where the power has dropped to 1/e (36.8 %); the half-power length is the 3 dB point.

Limits: T here is the internal transmittance. Reflection at the entry and exit faces (about 4 % per glass surface, 30 % for silicon; see the anti-reflection coating and Snell's law calculators) is not included, and neither are scattering, fluorescence, saturation at high intensity, or the deviations from Beer's law in concentrated solutions. Absorbance above about 2–3 is hard to measure in a spectrophotometer because so little light reaches the detector. Loss in a waveguide includes scattering from sidewall roughness; the exponential model still applies to the total.

FAQ

How do I convert dB/cm to absorption coefficient α?

Multiply by ln10/10 = 0.230259: α [cm⁻¹] = 0.2303 × loss [dB/cm]. Going back, 1 cm⁻¹ = 4.343 dB/cm. For dB/m, divide α in cm⁻¹ by 0.002303, or use α [m⁻¹] = 0.2303 × dB/m.

What is the difference between absorbance and optical density?

For a non-scattering sample they are the same number: A = OD = log₁₀(I₀/I). OD 1 transmits 10 %, OD 2 transmits 1 % and OD 3 transmits 0.1 %. Strictly, optical density measured through a scattering sample also includes light scattered out of the beam.

How do I use the Beer-Lambert law with molar absorptivity?

A = ε c L, with ε in L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹, c in mol/L and L in cm. Choose the molar mode, enter ε, the path length and the concentration in µmol/L. For example ε = 6220 at 50 µmol/L in a 1 cm cuvette gives A = 0.311.

What is penetration depth?

The depth 1/α at which the intensity has fallen to 1/e ≈ 36.8 % of its value just inside the surface. About 63 % of the light is absorbed within one penetration depth and 99 % within 4.6 penetration depths.

How is the extinction coefficient κ related to α?

α = 4πκ/λ₀ with λ₀ the vacuum wavelength. Silicon at 632.8 nm has κ ≈ 0.019, giving α ≈ 3.8×10³ cm⁻¹ and a penetration depth of about 2.6 µm.
