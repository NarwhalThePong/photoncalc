Worked example

A silicon strip-waveguide MZI at 1550 nm with ng = 4.2 and ΔL = 100 µm has FSR = (1.55 µm)² / (4.2 × 100 µm) ≈ 5.72 nm, or c / (4.2 × 100 µm) ≈ 714 GHz, and a differential delay of about 1.40 ps. With neff = 2.4 the phase difference is 2π × 154.84, so 1.677π after removing whole cycles: output A carries cos²(0.839π) ≈ 76.4 % and output B 23.6 %, and the nearest output-A peak sits at about 1549.08 nm. For a 100 GHz FSR (a common DWDM interleaver spacing) the arms must differ by c / (4.2 × 100 GHz) ≈ 714 µm.

How it works

An unbalanced Mach-Zehnder interferometer splits light into two arms of different length and recombines it. The extra length ΔL adds a phase difference Δφ = 2πneffΔL/λ, so the output swings between the two ports as the wavelength changes, giving a sinusoidal filter used for wavelength interleavers, filter stages, delay-line interferometers for DPSK demodulation and on-chip spectrometers.

The spacing between transmission peaks, the free spectral range, is set by the group index ng = neff − λ dneff/dλ, not the phase index, because it measures how fast the phase changes with wavelength. In high-contrast silicon waveguides ng (about 4.2) is far larger than neff (about 2.4), so using neff would overestimate the FSR by roughly 75 %. In frequency the FSR is simply the inverse of the differential group delay, c/(ngΔL). The phase and port powers use neff, because the absolute phase depends on the phase index.

Assumptions: an ideal 50:50 splitter and combiner, equal loss in both arms and a single polarization, so the extinction is perfect. Which physical port is cos² and which is sin² depends on the combiner (a Y-branch has only the cos² output; a 2×2 coupler adds a fixed phase between ports). The absolute phase is extremely sensitive to fabrication: a 1 nm error in a silicon waveguide's width shifts neff enough to move the peaks by around 1 nm, so treat the peak position as a nominal value and plan on a thermal phase shifter to tune it. The FSR itself is much more robust.

FAQ

How do I calculate the FSR of a Mach-Zehnder interferometer?

FSR = λ²/(n_g ΔL) in wavelength or c/(n_g ΔL) in frequency, with ΔL the arm length difference and n_g the group index of the arm waveguide. A silicon MZI with ΔL = 100 µm at 1550 nm has an FSR of about 5.7 nm.

Why use the group index instead of the effective index?

The FSR is the wavelength step that changes the phase difference by 2π, which depends on dφ/dλ. Differentiating 2πn_eff ΔL/λ including the dispersion of n_eff gives the group index n_g. The effective index sets the absolute peak positions, the group index sets their spacing.

What arm length difference gives a 100 GHz or 0.8 nm FSR?

ΔL = c/(n_g × FSR). In silicon (n_g ≈ 4.2) a 100 GHz FSR needs about 714 µm; in SiN (n_g ≈ 2.0) about 1.5 mm; in fiber (n_g ≈ 1.47) about 2.04 mm.

How is an MZI FSR different from a ring resonator FSR?

Same formula with a different length: for a ring the length is the full circumference, for an MZI it is the arm length difference. The MZI response is a smooth cos², whereas a ring gives narrow resonances whose sharpness is set by the coupling and loss.
