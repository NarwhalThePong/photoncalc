Worked example

The ⁸⁷Rb D2 line at 780.241 nm in a room-temperature vapour cell (25 °C): the Doppler FWHM is 509.7 MHz (1.035 pm, 0.0170 cm⁻¹), 84 times the 6.0666 MHz natural width, so the observed Voigt width is 513.0 MHz and saturated-absorption spectroscopy is needed to resolve the hyperfine structure. The Doppler width grows by 0.85 MHz per kelvin, and it would only shrink to the natural width at 42 mK. Caesium D2 at 20 °C gives 374.1 MHz, and a sodium cell at 200 °C gives 1653 MHz. A HeNe laser at 632.991 nm with a 400 K discharge has a 1518 MHz Doppler gain width, and a 30 cm cavity (FSR 499.7 MHz) fits 3.04 free spectral ranges under it, so 3 or 4 longitudinal modes can lase. A CO₂ laser line at 10.591 µm and 400 K is only 61.1 MHz wide by Doppler; with an illustrative pressure broadening of 7.5 MHz/Torr at 15 Torr (check the measured coefficient for your gas mix), the 112.5 MHz Lorentzian is larger than the Doppler width, the line is a mixed Voigt profile, and its width is 140.6 MHz, narrower than the 149.9 MHz FSR of a 1 m cavity. H-alpha from a 10 000 K gas has a thermal width of 32.6 GHz, or 46.8 pm (0.468 Å).

How it works

Atoms and molecules in a gas move in every direction with a Maxwell–Boltzmann spread of speeds, and each one sees the light Doppler-shifted by its velocity along the beam, ν = ν0(1 + vz/c). Because the line-of-sight velocity is Gaussian-distributed, the absorption or emission line becomes a Gaussian whose full width at half maximum is ΔνD = (ν0/c)√(8kBT ln2/m). It grows as the square root of temperature, shrinks as the square root of mass, and is a fixed fraction of the optical frequency, about 7.16×10⁻⁷ √(T/M) with T in kelvin and M in atomic mass units. That fixed fraction means the width in wavelength scales as λ, and in frequency as 1/λ: a CO₂ line at 10.6 µm is only tens of MHz wide, while a visible line from the same gas would be gigahertz wide.

Every atom also has a homogeneous, Lorentzian width that the Doppler effect cannot hide. The natural width Γ/2π = 1/(2πτ) comes from the finite lifetime τ of the excited state, about 6 MHz for the rubidium and potassium D2 lines. Collisions add pressure broadening, roughly linear in the pressure with a coefficient that depends on the gas pair. The real line is a convolution of the Gaussian and the Lorentzian, the Voigt profile, and the calculator gives its FWHM from the Olivero and Longbothum approximation, which is good to about 0.02 % of the exact Voigt width. The ratio ΔνD/fL tells you which regime you are in: alkali vapour cells at room temperature are Doppler dominated by a factor of about 80, which is why Doppler-free techniques such as saturated absorption or cold atoms are used to see hyperfine lines, while low-frequency molecular lines at tens of Torr are pressure dominated.

For gas lasers the Doppler width is the width of the gain curve. Dividing it by the cavity free spectral range c/(2nL) tells you how many longitudinal modes can sit under the gain line, so how short the cavity must be, or whether an etalon is needed, for single-frequency operation. The page also lists the most probable, mean and rms speeds and the rms velocity along the beam, the 1/e half-width ν0vp/c used in some textbooks, and the peak value of the normalized Gaussian line shape, which turns an integrated cross-section or line strength into a peak value.

Limits: the preset wavelengths and natural widths for Rb, Cs and Na come from Steck's alkali D-line data and for potassium from Tiecke's tables. The HeNe, CO₂ and H-alpha presets set only the wavelength and mass, because a laser or recombination line has no single natural width to quote; enter a homogeneous width with the custom option if you need one. The Doppler width is that of a single transition, so the hyperfine or isotope structure of a real vapour cell (for example the several components of natural rubidium) makes the observed absorption profile wider and asymmetric. The gas is assumed to be in thermal equilibrium with no bulk flow, beam collimation, wall effects or Dicke narrowing. Pressure broadening is linear in pressure with no pressure shift, power broadening and transit-time broadening are not included (add them to the Lorentzian width if they matter), and the Voigt approximation treats all homogeneous mechanisms as one Lorentzian. The mode count assumes an empty-cavity FSR and ignores mode pulling and spatial hole burning.

FAQ

What is the Doppler width of the rubidium D2 line at room temperature?

About 510 MHz FWHM for ⁸⁷Rb at 25 °C (780.241 nm, mass 86.909 u), or 1.035 pm. ⁸⁵Rb is about 1.2 % wider (515.7 MHz) because it is lighter. The natural width is 6.07 MHz, so the Doppler width is about 84 times larger.

How do I calculate Doppler broadening?

Use Δν_D = (ν₀/c)√(8 k_B T ln2/m), where ν₀ is the line frequency, T the temperature in kelvin and m the mass of the atom or molecule. In practical units Δν_D/ν₀ ≈ 7.16×10⁻⁷ √(T/M) with M in atomic mass units, and the width in wavelength is Δλ = λ²Δν/c, or equivalently Δλ/λ = Δν/ν₀.

What is the Doppler linewidth of a HeNe laser?

About 1.5 GHz at 632.8 nm for a discharge near 400 K (1518 MHz with ²⁰Ne at 127 °C). A 30 cm cavity has a 500 MHz free spectral range, so three or four longitudinal modes fit under the gain curve, which is why short or etalon-stabilized HeNe lasers are needed for single-frequency output.

How do I combine Doppler and natural or pressure broadening?

The two mechanisms convolve into a Voigt profile. Its FWHM is well approximated by f_V ≈ 0.5346 f_L + √(0.2166 f_L² + f_G²), where f_L is the total Lorentzian FWHM (natural plus pressure) and f_G the Doppler FWHM (Olivero and Longbothum 1977, accurate to about 0.02 %). Simply adding the widths overestimates the result, and adding them in quadrature is only right for two Gaussians.

Does Doppler broadening depend on wavelength?

In frequency it scales with the line frequency, so it is proportional to 1/λ. In wavelength it is proportional to λ. The fractional width Δν_D/ν₀ depends only on temperature and mass, about 1.33×10⁻⁶ for rubidium at room temperature and 7.13×10⁻⁵ for hydrogen at 10 000 K.

Why is my measured line wider than the Doppler width?

Common reasons are unresolved hyperfine or isotope components, pressure broadening from a buffer gas, power broadening from a strong probe, the linewidth of the laser itself and transit-time broadening in a small beam.
