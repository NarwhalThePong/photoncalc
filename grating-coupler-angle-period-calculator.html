Formula

n_c sin θ = n_eff − m λ / Λ    (Bragg / phase-matching condition)
air side: sin θ_air = n_c sin θ = n_eff − m λ / Λ
Λ_target = m λ / (n_eff − n_c sin θ_target)    dθ/dλ ≈ −m / (Λ n_c cos θ)  (n_eff held fixed)

Worked example

A shallow-etch silicon grating with n_eff = 2.75 and Λ = 630 nm at 1550 nm under oxide: n_c sin θ = 2.75 − 1550/630 = 0.2897, so θ = 11.57° in the oxide and 16.84° in air. Hitting an 8° angle in oxide needs Λ = 1550 / (2.75 − 1.444 sin 8°) = 608.1 nm.

How it works

A grating coupler scatters light out of a waveguide at the angle where the grating's momentum makes up the difference between the guided mode and a free-space wave. Fibers are normally tilted 8–15° off vertical so that the second-order back-reflection does not couple back into the waveguide.

The air-side angle depends only on n_eff, λ and Λ, because Snell's law at the cladding surface cancels the cladding index. The tuning estimate holds n_eff fixed, so real devices (where n_eff also changes with λ) tune somewhat differently. Get n_eff for a partially etched grating from a mode solver, usually as an average of the etched and unetched sections weighted by fill factor.

FAQ

Why not couple vertically?

At 0° the grating also reflects strongly back along the waveguide (second-order Bragg reflection), which hurts efficiency and causes ripple.

What n_eff should I use?

For a 220 nm silicon platform with a 70 nm shallow etch, values around 2.7–2.85 at 1550 nm are typical; use your mode solver for a real design.

Does this give efficiency?

No. Efficiency depends on directionality, mode overlap with the fiber and reflections, which need a full simulation.
