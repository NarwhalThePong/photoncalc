Formula

d = m·λ₀ / (4n₁), m = 1, 3, 5 …    ideal index n₁ = √(n₀·ns)
R(λ₀) = [(n₀ns − n₁²) / (n₀ns + n₁²)]²    uncoated R = [(n₀ − ns) / (n₀ + ns)]²
R(λ) = (r₁² + r₂² + 2r₁r₂cos2δ) / (1 + r₁²r₂² + 2r₁r₂cos2δ),   r₁ = (n₀ − n₁)/(n₀ + n₁), r₂ = (n₁ − ns)/(n₁ + ns), δ = 2πn₁d/λ

Worked example

A quarter-wave of magnesium fluoride (n₁ = 1.38) on BK7 glass (ns = 1.52) designed for 550 nm is d = 550 / (4 × 1.38) = 99.64 nm thick. Uncoated glass reflects ((1 − 1.52)/(1 + 1.52))² = 4.26 % per surface; the coating brings that down to ((1.52 − 1.9044)/(1.52 + 1.9044))² = 1.26 % at 550 nm, rising to 1.62 % at 450 nm and 1.60 % at 700 nm. A window coated on both sides transmits 97.5 % instead of 91.8 %. MgF₂ cannot reach zero on glass because the ideal index √1.52 = 1.233 is lower than any durable coating material. On silicon at 1550 nm (ns = 3.48) a 207.2 nm silicon nitride layer with n₁ = 1.87, almost exactly √3.48 = 1.865, cuts the reflectance from 30.6 % to 0.0006 %.

How it works

Every interface between two indices reflects part of the light: about 4 % for glass in air and over 30 % for silicon. A thin film between the two media creates a second reflection, from its lower surface. When the film is a quarter of a wavelength thick (optically), the light reflected from the bottom travels an extra half wavelength and comes back out of phase with the reflection from the top surface, so the two cancel. They cancel completely only if both reflections are equally strong, which happens when the coating index is the geometric mean √(n₀·ns) of the surrounding indices.

The calculator uses the exact single-layer result at normal incidence: the two Fresnel amplitude coefficients are summed with all multiple reflections inside the film (the Airy sum), which is the same answer the thin-film characteristic-matrix method gives. Away from the design wavelength the film is no longer a quarter wave and the reflectance climbs back toward the uncoated value, which is why single-layer coatings work best over a limited band and why broadband coatings use two or more layers. A 3λ/4 layer gives the same reflectance at the design wavelength but a band about three times narrower.

Limits: normal incidence only (at oblique angles the minimum shifts to shorter wavelengths and splits between s and p polarization), no absorption, and the indices are treated as constant with wavelength. The window transmission assumes two identical coated surfaces, a non-absorbing substrate and incoherent multiple reflections between the two faces.

FAQ

How thick should an anti-reflection coating be?

A quarter of the wavelength inside the coating: d = λ₀ / (4n₁). For MgF₂ (n = 1.38) at 550 nm that is 99.6 nm. Odd multiples (3λ/4, 5λ/4) also work at the design wavelength but over a narrower band.

What is the ideal index for an AR coating?

The geometric mean of the two media, n₁ = √(n₀·n_s). For glass in air (n_s = 1.52) that is 1.233, for silicon (3.48) it is 1.865, which is close to silicon nitride.

Why does MgF₂ only reduce glass reflectance to about 1.3 %?

Its index of 1.38 is higher than the ideal 1.23, so the two reflections do not cancel completely. The residual is ((1.52 − 1.38²)/(1.52 + 1.38²))² ≈ 1.26 % per surface at the design wavelength.

What happens at other wavelengths?

The layer is no longer exactly a quarter wave, so the cancellation is incomplete and the reflectance rises toward the uncoated value. Enter a second wavelength to see it; for MgF₂ on glass designed at 550 nm it is about 1.6 % at 450 and 700 nm.

Does this work for solar cells and silicon photonics?

Yes, at normal incidence. A silicon nitride quarter-wave layer is the standard AR coating on silicon solar cells and on cleaved silicon facets, with an index near the ideal √n_Si.
