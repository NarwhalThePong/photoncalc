Formula

PASE = NF · hν · G · Bref per amplifier, with G = span loss    Bref = c·Δλref / λ²
OSNR = Pch / (N · NF · hν · G · Bref)
OSNR[dB] = Pch[dBm] − Lspan − NF − 10·log₁₀N − 10·log₁₀(hν·Bref / 1 mW)    (≈ 58 + Pch − Lspan − NF − 10·log₁₀N at 1550 nm, 0.1 nm)
Nmax = ⌊10(OSNR1 span − OSNRreq)/10⌋

Worked example

At 1550 nm a photon carries hν = 1.282×10⁻¹⁹ J and the 0.1 nm reference bandwidth is c·0.1 nm / (1550 nm)² ≈ 12.48 GHz, so hν·Bref ≈ 1.60 nW = −57.96 dBm: this is the “58” in the 58 dB rule. Launching 0 dBm per channel into ten 80 km spans of 20 dB loss with 5 dB noise-figure amplifiers gives OSNR ≈ 57.96 + 0 − 20 − 5 − 10 = 22.96 dB in 0.1 nm. Each amplifier adds about −32.96 dBm of ASE in the reference bandwidth and ten of them −22.96 dBm. A single span would give 32.96 dB, so a receiver that needs 15 dB has 7.96 dB of margin and the same spans could be repeated up to ⌊101.796⌋ = 62 times (ignoring nonlinearity). With 40 channels the total launch power is 16.0 dBm.

How it works

Every optical amplifier adds amplified spontaneous emission (ASE), broadband noise that travels with the signal and cannot be filtered out within the channel. The optical signal-to-noise ratio is the channel power divided by the ASE power in a fixed reference bandwidth, conventionally 0.1 nm (about 12.5 GHz at 1550 nm), so that values from different systems and optical spectrum analyzers can be compared. It is the main figure of merit for long-haul and DWDM links: receivers and transponders specify a required OSNR, for example around 12–18 dB in 0.1 nm for common formats, and the link must deliver more than that with margin.

For a chain of identical spans in which each amplifier exactly makes up the loss of the span before it, each amplifier contributes the same ASE, NF·hν·G·Bref, and the noise powers add. That gives the well-known rule OSNR ≈ 58 + Pch − Lspan − NF − 10·log₁₀N: every doubling of the number of spans costs 3 dB, and each extra dB of span loss or noise figure costs one dB. The calculator computes the constant from the wavelength and reference bandwidth instead of using 58 directly. It follows that many short spans are much better than a few long ones, because span loss enters in dB while the span count enters only logarithmically.

Limits: the formula uses the high-gain approximation (ASE ∝ NF·G rather than NF·G − 1, an error of under 0.1 dB for gains above 15 dB), identical spans, flat gain and noise figure, and amplifier output power equal to the launch power. It ignores fiber nonlinearity, which stops you from simply raising the launch power, as well as the transmitter's own OSNR, gain tilt, filtering and polarization-dependent effects, so real links are usually designed with a few dB of extra OSNR margin. Convert to OSNR in the signal bandwidth by subtracting 10·log₁₀(Bsignal/12.5 GHz).

FAQ

How do you calculate OSNR?

For a link of N identical amplified spans, OSNR in 0.1 nm ≈ 58 + P_ch − L_span − NF − 10·log₁₀N, with launch power per channel in dBm and span loss and noise figure in dB. The 58 is −10·log₁₀(hν·B_ref / 1 mW) for 1550 nm light and a 0.1 nm (12.5 GHz) reference bandwidth.

Why is OSNR quoted in a 0.1 nm bandwidth?

ASE noise is broadband, so its power depends on the bandwidth you measure it in. Fixing the reference bandwidth at 0.1 nm, a common optical spectrum analyzer resolution, makes OSNR numbers comparable between systems regardless of the signal's own bandwidth.

How much OSNR do I lose by doubling the number of spans?

3 dB. The ASE from N identical amplifiers adds, so the OSNR falls by 10·log₁₀N: 10 spans cost 10 dB and 100 spans cost 20 dB compared with a single span.

What is a typical EDFA noise figure?

Commercial erbium-doped fiber amplifiers are typically 4.5–6 dB. The quantum limit for a high-gain phase-insensitive amplifier is 3 dB; distributed Raman amplification gives a lower effective noise figure.

Can I just increase the launch power to improve OSNR?

Only up to a point. Each extra dB of launch power raises the OSNR by a dB, but beyond a few dBm per channel fiber nonlinearities (self- and cross-phase modulation, four-wave mixing) add distortion that this calculator does not model.
