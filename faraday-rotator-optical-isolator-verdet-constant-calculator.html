Formula

Worked example

A TGG Faraday isolator for 1064 nm: the Verdet fit gives |V| = 37.5 rad/(T·m) (0.129 arcmin/(Oe·cm)), so in a 1 T magnet a 45° rotation needs a 20.9 mm rod. With 40 dB polarizers the isolation at the design point is 40 dB, set entirely by the polarizers. The rotation drifts by −0.104°/nm and −0.152°/K, and 30 dB of isolation survives from 1047.8 to 1081.0 nm and from 11.1 to 33.7 °C (a ±1.72° window). Heat the same rod to 60 °C and the rotation falls to 39.9° (V ∝ 1/T), cutting the isolation to 20.9 dB. Used at 633 nm, the same 20.9 mm rod rotates 156° because |V| rises to 130.6 rad/(T·m), and a HeNe isolator needs only 6.0 mm at 1 T. A TSAG rotator of 15 mm at 1030 nm (|V| = 52.3) needs 1.00 T, and a Tb₂O₃ ceramic (|V| = 128.5 at 1064 nm) reaches 45° in only 5.1 mm at 1.2 T. With 50 dB polarizers and a 40 dB target, the TGG window shrinks to ±0.54°, which is 1058.8–1069.3 nm or 18.5–25.6 °C.

How it works

A magnetic field along a light beam turns the plane of linear polarization as the light travels through glass or a crystal: the Faraday effect. The angle grows linearly with the field and the path, θ = VBL, where the Verdet constant V is a property of the material at a given wavelength and temperature. Unlike the optical activity of quartz or sugar, or the rotation from a half-wave plate, Faraday rotation is non-reciprocal: its sense is fixed by the direction of B, not by the direction of travel. Light that passes through and is reflected back is rotated twice the same way, 2θ in total, instead of being unwound to zero. That is what makes optical isolators, circulators and Faraday mirrors possible.

A free-space isolator is a polarizer, a 45° Faraday rotator and a second polarizer at 45°. Forward light leaves the rotator aligned with the exit polarizer and passes. Light reflected back enters through the exit polarizer at 45°, is rotated another 45° in the same sense, and reaches the entrance polarizer at 90°, where it is blocked. Two things limit how well it blocks. The first is the rotation error δ = θ − 45°: the leaked fraction is sin²δ, so 30 dB needs |δ| ≤ 1.8° and 40 dB needs |δ| ≤ 0.57°. The second is the extinction ratio of the polarizers, which caps the isolation even at perfect rotation. The calculator propagates the beam both ways through partial polarizers and gives the isolation, the forward loss from the rotation error, and the band of wavelength and temperature over which the isolation stays above your target.

The Verdet constant of the terbium and cerium materials used in laser isolators is paramagnetic. It falls steeply with wavelength, roughly as 1/(λ² − λ₀²) away from the absorption resonance at λ₀, and the presets use the single-transition fits F, G, λ₀ tabulated by Vojna et al. (2019) for the 400–1100 nm region. It also falls with temperature, following the Curie–Weiss law 1/(T − Tw) (Barnes and Petway 1992 measured this for TGG). Near room temperature a 45° rotator therefore loses about 0.15° per kelvin, whatever the material. This is why isolators are specified at one wavelength and temperature, are tuned by sliding the rod in the magnet, and lose isolation when they warm up under high average power. Note the sign convention: TGG has a negative Verdet constant (it rotates clockwise when the beam travels along B). The calculator works with |V| and the magnitude of θ, because the sense of rotation does not change the isolation.

Limits: the presets are room-temperature single-transition fits for 400–1100 nm, so a warning appears outside that range. Sample-to-sample and crystal-to-ceramic differences are a few percent, and the absolute value of the room temperature is not specified, so always trim a real rotator by measurement. The temperature scaling applies the Curie–Weiss factor to the whole Verdet constant, which ignores the small temperature-independent mixing term. B is the field component along the beam averaged over the rod (θ = V∫B dz); permanent-magnet fields are strongly non-uniform, so use the measured or simulated average. The model is linear and far from saturation. It does not describe saturated ferrimagnetic garnets (YIG and Bi-substituted iron garnets in telecom isolators), whose rotation per length is fixed once they are magnetized and does not scale with B. Absorption, AR-coating loss, polarization-independent (walk-off or wedge) fiber isolators, cascaded two-stage isolators, and the thermal lensing and thermally induced depolarization that limit kilowatt isolators (Snetkov et al. 2014) are not modeled. The back-reflected light is assumed to be polarized along the exit polarizer, as a reflection of the forward beam would be.

FAQ

What is the Verdet constant of TGG?

About 37.5 rad/(T·m) in magnitude at 1064 nm and 130.6 rad/(T·m) at 633 nm at room temperature, from the fit |V| = 820.3 × 239.3²/(λ² − 239.3²) − 6.2 rad/(T·m) with λ in nm (Vojna et al. 2019). That is 0.129 and 0.449 arcmin/(Oe·cm). Its sign is negative by the usual convention, and it scales roughly as 1/T with temperature.

How long should a TGG rod be for a 1064 nm Faraday isolator?

L = (π/4)/(VB). With |V| = 37.5 rad/(T·m) and a 1 T magnet, L = 20.9 mm. At 1.5 T it is 14.0 mm. At 633 nm, a 1 T magnet needs only 6.0 mm because the Verdet constant is 3.5 times larger there.

Why does an optical isolator use 45° of rotation?

Forward light is turned 45° to line up with the exit polarizer. Reflected light passes the rotator a second time and, because Faraday rotation is non-reciprocal, is turned another 45° the same way, so it arrives at the entrance polarizer at 90° and is blocked. A half-wave plate would rotate the returning light back to 0° and give no isolation.

How much isolation do I lose if the rotation is not exactly 45°?

The leaked fraction is about sin²δ plus the polarizer leakage, where δ is the rotation error. With ideal polarizers, 30 dB needs |δ| ≤ 1.81° and 40 dB needs |δ| ≤ 0.57°. With 40 dB polarizers, keeping 30 dB needs |δ| ≤ 1.72°. For a 20.9 mm TGG rotator at 1 T, designed for 1064 nm and 22 °C, that window spans about 1048–1081 nm, or 11–34 °C.

Does Faraday rotation depend on temperature?

Yes, strongly for paramagnetic materials such as TGG, TSAG and terbium glasses. Their Verdet constant follows the Curie–Weiss law, V ∝ 1/(T − T_w), so near room temperature a 45° rotator loses about 0.15° per kelvin, and cooling to cryogenic temperatures raises V several-fold. Diamagnetic glasses such as fused silica have a much smaller and nearly temperature-independent Verdet constant.

Can I use this for a fiber or telecom isolator at 1550 nm?

Only for paramagnetic rotators. Most 1310 and 1550 nm isolators use magnetically saturated iron-garnet films (YIG or Bi-substituted garnets), whose rotation is a fixed number of degrees per millimetre once saturated and does not follow θ = VBL. For those, use the manufacturer's specific rotation. The TGG fit extrapolated to 1550 nm gives about 13.8 rad/(T·m), but the presets are only fitted from 400 to 1100 nm.
