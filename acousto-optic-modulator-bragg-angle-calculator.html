Worked example

A TeO₂ modulator for a 633 nm HeNe beam driven at 110 MHz (longitudinal sound, v = 4200 m/s) has an acoustic wavelength of 38.18 µm. The Bragg angle is 3.668 mrad inside the crystal and 8.289 mrad (0.475°) in air, so the first order leaves 16.58 mrad (0.950°) from the zero order, about 1.66 mm apart after 10 cm, and is shifted up or down in frequency by exactly 110 MHz (147 fm in wavelength). With a 10 mm long, 1 mm high transducer the Klein–Cook parameter is Q = 12.1, inside the Bragg regime. The two-wave model needs 0.581 W of acoustic power for 100 % diffraction and 0.145 W for 50 %, and gives 98.7 % at 0.5 W (a 13-order coupled-wave calculation at this Q gives 95.3 %: some light leaks into the second order). Focusing the beam to 0.2 mm gives a 47.6 ns acoustic transit time, a 30.5 ns 10–90 % rise time and 15.7 MHz of modulation bandwidth. The same crystal at 1064 nm and 80 MHz with a 20 mm transducer needs 0.820 W for full efficiency, and the 1064 nm beam is deflected by 20.27 mrad. Germanium at 10.6 µm (40 MHz, L = 40 mm, H = 5 mm) needs 39 W for 100 %, because the required power grows as λ². Used as a deflector, TeO₂ with a 5 mm beam (τ = 1.19 µs) scanned over 40 MHz sweeps 6.03 mrad and resolves 47.6 spots.

How it works

An acousto-optic modulator (AOM) is a transparent crystal or glass with a piezoelectric transducer bonded to one side. The RF drive launches a sound wave that writes a moving refractive-index grating of period Λ = v/f into the material. Light that meets this grating at the Bragg angle is diffracted into a first order, just as X-rays reflect off crystal planes, and because the grating moves, the diffracted light is Doppler-shifted by exactly the drive frequency: up for the +1 order, down for the −1 order. Switching the RF on and off switches the first-order beam on and off; changing the RF power sets how much light is diffracted; and changing the RF frequency steers the beam. These three uses (pulse picker or Q-switch, intensity or frequency shifter, deflector) all come from the same few formulas, which this calculator evaluates together.

The Bragg angle is small (a few mrad) because the sound wavelength is tens of microns. Inside the crystal sin θB = λ/(2nΛ); outside, after refraction at the face, the index cancels and the angle is λ/(2Λ), which is what you align in the lab. The Klein–Cook parameter Q = 2πλL/(nΛ²) tells you whether the cell is thick enough to behave as a Bragg grating with one useful order (Q ≳ 2π) or acts as a thin phase grating with many orders (Raman–Nath, Q ≲ 1). The efficiency formula is the exact solution of the two coupled waves at Bragg incidence: η rises as sin² of the square root of acoustic power, with a material figure of merit M₂ = n⁶p²/(ρv³) that rewards high index, strong photoelastic constant p and slow sound. That is why TeO₂ beats fused silica by a factor of over 20, and why infrared AOMs need tens of watts, because the required power scales as λ².

Speed is set by the time the sound takes to cross the light beam. For a Gaussian beam the 10–90 % rise time is 0.641 d/v, the familiar rule; smaller beams switch faster but diverge more, and once the optical divergence approaches the acoustic divergence the efficiency drops. The Bragg bandwidth shows how far the drive frequency can move before the fixed incidence angle no longer satisfies the Bragg condition; the calculator solves the quadratic phase mismatch rather than only the linearized 1.8 nv²/(λfL) rule, which overestimates the band when it is comparable to the centre frequency. In practice the transducer's electrical bandwidth is often narrower, so for a deflector enter the real scan range: the number of resolvable spots is the time-bandwidth product τΔf.

Limits: this is the isotropic Bragg model with plane acoustic waves and a lossless medium. It does not cover anisotropic (birefringent) diffraction such as the slow-shear TeO₂ deflectors and tunable filters, which follow Dixon's equations, nor acoustic attenuation, beam steering transducers, thermal effects or the efficiency loss when optical and acoustic divergences are mismatched. Material constants are typical literature values at 633 nm (10.6 µm for Ge); M₂ depends on wavelength and polarization, so use your device datasheet for design-grade numbers. Efficiency in the intermediate regime (1 < Q < 2π) is lower than shown because light leaks into higher orders.

FAQ

How do I calculate the Bragg angle of an AOM?

Divide the acoustic velocity by the drive frequency to get the sound wavelength Λ = v/f, then θ = λ/(2Λ) in air (in radians, small angle) or λ/(2nΛ) inside the crystal. TeO₂ at 110 MHz has Λ = 4200/110 MHz = 38.18 µm, so a 633 nm beam needs 8.29 mrad (0.475°) in air.

How much does an AOM shift the laser frequency?

Exactly the RF drive frequency, up for the +1 order and down for the −1 order: an 80 MHz AOM shifts 1064 nm light by ±80 MHz, a wavelength change of λ²f/c = 0.30 pm. A double pass gives twice the drive frequency, which is how cold-atom and spectroscopy setups tune lasers.

What is the rise time of an acousto-optic modulator?

About the acoustic transit time across the beam: for a Gaussian beam with 1/e² diameter d, t₁₀₋₉₀ = 0.641 d/v. In TeO₂ (4200 m/s) a 0.2 mm beam switches in 30.5 ns and a 1 mm beam in 153 ns, so AOMs are focused hard for fast pulse picking.

What is the Klein–Cook parameter Q?

Q = 2πλL/(nΛ²) compares the length of the interaction with the distance over which a diffracted order walks out of the grating. For Q ≳ 2π the cell works in the Bragg regime with a single strong first order; for Q ≲ 1 it acts as a thin grating with many orders (Raman–Nath) and the Bragg formulas no longer apply.

How much RF power does an AOM need?

For 100 % first-order efficiency P = λ²H/(2M₂L), where M₂ is the material figure of merit and L/H the transducer aspect ratio. A TeO₂ cell with L = 10 mm and H = 1 mm needs 0.58 W at 633 nm; the same geometry in fused silica needs about 13 W. Power scales as λ², so 10.6 µm germanium AOMs need tens of watts. Acoustic power is the electrical RF power times the transducer conversion efficiency.

How many resolvable spots does an acousto-optic deflector have?

N = τΔf, the acoustic transit time across the aperture times the scan bandwidth. A 5 mm beam in TeO₂ (τ = 1.19 µs) scanned over 40 MHz gives about 48 spots and sweeps λΔf/v = 6.0 mrad at 633 nm. Slow-shear TeO₂ deflectors reach hundreds of spots by using the slower acoustic wave.

What is the difference between an AOM and an EOM?

An AOM diffracts light with a sound grating, giving a frequency-shifted first order, MHz-scale switching and low drive voltage. An electro-optic modulator changes the phase through the Pockels effect with no frequency shift and switches in picoseconds, but needs high voltage (or long waveguides).
